# Reader-facing voice

Write for someone deciding what OSE means for their life and what they could do
with other people. Explain the purpose and the work in ordinary language.

For every sentence, ask what the reader learns from it. Name the people, task,
object or decision. Explain a connection when it matters: how a drawing helps
someone make a part, or how production could pay for a teaching program.

Remove these patterns during editorial review:

- Empty contrasts: “a question rather than a promise,” “not just X, but Y.”
  State the useful point directly. Keep distinctions that explain a real choice.
- Commentary about the text: “this introduction frames,” “the story widens,”
  “a living introduction.” Give the reader the subject itself.
- Advice about the writing embedded in the story: “A responsible story keeps
  that distinction visible.” Delete it. Keep the substantive distinction itself.
- Generic reassurance or caution. Keep concrete limitations next to the relevant
  fact, such as which economics need testing or which drawings need review.

Project examples serve the explanation. A rover can illustrate a build with
friends; a microfactory can illustrate a larger ambition. Neither needs to be
the backbone of an introduction to OSE.

Read the whole passage aloud after revising. Phrase checks catch old wording;
they cannot determine whether a new sentence is worth the reader's time.

The user identified self-commentary and empty negation as the main problem.
Metaphor and abstract language are not blanket prohibitions. Preserve language
that helps the reader; avoid replacing the whole voice to fix a local issue.
